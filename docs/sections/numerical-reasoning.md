# Numerical reasoning

**In one line:** you read a table or chart of business data and pick the one correct answer out of five, usually after a short calculation.

Numerical reasoning is one of the three abilities in Verify G+. SHL's fact sheet says it measures solving problems with numerical data, using the right mathematical method, and interpreting data in charts, graphs and tables. Ten of the test's 30 questions are numerical.

## What it measures

SHL's own description is about making "correct decisions or inferences" from numbers, not about speed with arithmetic. In practice the questions check three things:

- **Finding the right figure.** Most tables hold far more data than one question needs. Picking the right row, column and unit is half the work.
- **Choosing the right operation.** Percentage change, share of a total, a weighted average, a rate per person. Each has one correct method and one tempting wrong one.
- **Working through two or three steps** without losing track, for example "apply a price rise, then a fall in volume, then compare".

The maths itself rarely goes beyond what you used at school: percentages, ratios, averages and simple multiplication. What makes it hard is doing it accurately, under time pressure, on data you have never seen.

## How the questions look

Each question shows a table or chart, a question underneath and five answer options. Several questions can share one data set, so time spent understanding a table pays off more than once.

| Part | What you see |
|---|---|
| Data | A table, bar chart, line graph or pie chart, often with a title, units and footnotes |
| Question | One task, such as "What was the percentage increase..." or "Which region..." |
| Options | Five choices, usually numbers. Wrong options are often the answer you get from a common mistake. |

Units matter. A table headed "units (thousands)" means a figure of 42 is 42,000. A chart in percentages cannot be added up like counts.

SHL's support pages say you may use a calculator for a numerical test unless you are told otherwise, and that if the test shows its own on-screen calculator, that is the one to use. Pen and paper are fine. Check your invitation, and have both ready.

## A method that works

1. **Read the question before the data.** Know what you are looking for, then go and find it.
2. **Check the title, units and footnotes.** Thousands or millions, per month or per year, percentages or counts.
3. **Write down only the figures you need.** Two or three numbers on paper beat holding them in your head.
4. **Name the operation out loud.** "Change divided by the old value." "Part divided by the total." This one habit stops most errors.
5. **Estimate first.** Round the numbers and get a rough answer. If two options are close to your estimate, work it exactly. If only one is, you can often pick it and move on.
6. **Check against the options.** If your answer is not listed, you probably used the wrong base or unit. Look for the option that matches a common slip, then avoid it.

## Traps that cost points

- **Wrong base for a percentage.** Percentage change is always divided by the *starting* value. A rise from 42 to 59 is 17 ÷ 42, not 17 ÷ 59.
- **Averaging averages.** If departments have different headcounts, you cannot just average their per-person figures. Weight each by its size.
- **Adding percentages across different totals.** A 10% rise in one region and a 20% rise in another is not a 30% rise overall.
- **Missing the unit.** Answers given in units when the table is in thousands, or in pounds when it asks for pence.
- **Reading the wrong row.** Busy tables invite it. Put a finger, or the cursor, on the row label as you read across.
- **Doing more work than needed.** "Which month had the most orders" only needs a comparison. You do not have to calculate every exact figure if one is clearly highest.

## Timing

Verify G+ allows 36 minutes for up to 30 questions across all three abilities, so a little over a minute per question on average. SHL does not publish a separate time per ability, so think in terms of the whole test. Numerical items tend to take longer than inductive ones, so you need to bank time elsewhere. As our guidance: if you have spent 90 seconds on one numerical question and are still not close, make your best estimate, choose, and move on.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from Verify G+ or any SHL material. Use a calculator and paper, as you would in the real test. Answer every question in a data set before opening the explanations.

### Data set 1: Regional sales

A stationery wholesaler's sales, in units (thousands), for 2025.

| Region | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | 42 | 48 | 51 | 59 |
| South | 36 | 33 | 39 | 42 |
| East | 25 | 30 | 28 | 37 |
| West | 50 | 46 | 52 | 62 |

**Item 1.** By what percentage did North's sales rise from Q1 to Q4?

A. 17.0% &nbsp; B. 28.8% &nbsp; C. 35.0% &nbsp; D. 40.5% &nbsp; E. 71.2%

**Item 2.** In which quarter did East account for the largest share of that quarter's total sales?

A. Q1 &nbsp; B. Q2 &nbsp; C. Q3 &nbsp; D. Q4 &nbsp; E. Cannot say from the data

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 and 2</summary>

**Item 1: D, 40.5%.** The rise is 59 − 42 = 17. Divide by the starting value: 17 ÷ 42 = 0.405, so 40.5%. *Why not B:* 28.8% is 17 ÷ 59, which uses the end value as the base. *Why not E:* 71.2% is 42 ÷ 59, which is not a change at all.

**Item 2: B, Q2.** Quarter totals are 153, 157, 170 and 200. East's shares are 25 ÷ 153 = 16.3%, 30 ÷ 157 = 19.1%, 28 ÷ 170 = 16.5% and 37 ÷ 200 = 18.5%. Q2 is highest. *Why not D:* Q4 has East's biggest figure, 37, but the total is also much bigger, so its share is smaller.

</details>

### Data set 2: Staff training

A company's four departments and their training records for the year.

| Department | Headcount | Completed compliance course | Average training hours per employee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finance | 80 | 95% | 12 |
| Operations | 240 | 70% | 6 |
| Sales | 150 | 84% | 9 |
| IT | 60 | 90% | 15 |

**Item 3.** What is the average number of training hours per employee across the whole company, to one decimal place?

A. 8.3 &nbsp; B. 8.8 &nbsp; C. 10.5 &nbsp; D. 11.0 &nbsp; E. 42.0

**Item 4.** Which department accounts for the largest share of the company's total training hours?

A. Finance &nbsp; B. Operations &nbsp; C. Sales &nbsp; D. IT &nbsp; E. Sales and Operations equally

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 3 and 4</summary>

**Item 3: B, 8.8.** Total hours: 80 × 12 = 960, 240 × 6 = 1,440, 150 × 9 = 1,350, 60 × 15 = 900. That is 4,650 hours for 530 people, so 4,650 ÷ 530 = 8.77, which rounds to 8.8. *Why not C:* 10.5 is the plain average of 12, 6, 9 and 15. It ignores that Operations, with the lowest hours, is by far the biggest department.

**Item 4: B, Operations.** Operations has 1,440 hours, the most of any department, even though its hours per person are the lowest. Its size outweighs the low average. *Why not D:* IT has the highest hours per person but the fewest people.

</details>

### Data set 3: Online shop

Monthly figures for an online shop, January to June.

| Month | Visitors (thousands) | Conversion rate (share of visitors who place an order) |
|---|---|---|
| January | 120 | 2.5% |
| February | 110 | 3.0% |
| March | 150 | 2.0% |
| April | 160 | 2.4% |
| May | 140 | 3.5% |
| June | 180 | 2.5% |

**Item 5.** In July, visitors are 10% higher than in June and the conversion rate is 2.2%. How many orders are placed in July?

A. 3,960 &nbsp; B. 4,356 &nbsp; C. 4,500 &nbsp; D. 4,792 &nbsp; E. 4,950

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, item 5</summary>

**Item 5: B, 4,356.** July visitors: 180 × 1.10 = 198 thousand. Orders: 198,000 × 0.022 = 4,356. *Why not A:* 3,960 applies 2.2% to June's 180,000 visitors and forgets the rise. *Why not E:* 4,950 applies June's 2.5% to July's visitors.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | D | B | B | B | B |

- **5:** your method is solid. Work on speed: redo the set with a 5-minute limit.
- **4:** look at what your misses have in common. Base errors (items 1, 2) and weighting errors (items 3, 4) are the usual pair. Reread the traps list.
- **3 or fewer:** slow down and say the operation out loud before you calculate, every time, until it is automatic. Then rebuild speed.
